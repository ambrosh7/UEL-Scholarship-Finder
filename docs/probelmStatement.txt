UEL Fit & Scholarship Finder

The Scenario
We help the University of East London attract students from India. We do this mainly through short videos
on Instagram and YouTube, posts by social media influencers, and online sessions for parents.
All of that points students to one place: an online tool called the UEL Fit & Scholarship Finder. A student
answers a few questions about themselves on their phone. In return, they find out:
• which UEL course suits them, and whether they appear to meet its entry requirements;
• what scholarship they could get, and roughly what their first year would really cost;

• what their life as a student in London could look like.
This is not a course search. The Finder sells UEL to the student, and it does that through two hooks.
• The scholarship. UEL offers international students money off their tuition fees, whether they are
starting a bachelor’s or a master’s. For most Indian families, cost is the biggest question, so the
scholarship is what gets a student to start the Finder and to finish it. Showing them, early and honestly,
how much they could save turns “I could never afford London” into “maybe I can”.
• Their life in London. Every university website lists courses and facts. We want the student to see
themselves there: leaving home, arriving in London, studying their subject, spending their weekends
on the things they love, and where the degree could take them. Built from their own answers, this is
what makes them want UEL, not just qualify for it.
The course match underneath gives them confidence that they could actually get in. Together, the three
take a student from “just looking” to wanting to apply.
To see their full results, the student gives us their name and phone number. We then follow up on
WhatsApp, and one of our advisers calls the students who are most likely to apply, or who need to apply
soon. With the student’s permission, their details are passed to UEL, who decide whether to offer them a
place.
Who uses it, and what they want to know.
• Students, applying for a bachelor’s or a master’s degree: “Would I get in, can I afford it, and what
would it lead to?”
• Parents: “Is it worth the money, and will my child be safe and looked after?”
• UEL: more good students who actually apply. Many Indian students apply through paid education
agents, and UEL pays the agent a commission, often £2,000 or more per student. Every student who
comes through the Finder instead is one UEL does not pay an agent for.
Things worth knowing.
• In most Indian families, parents help decide where their child studies, and usually help pay for it.
• Most students will open the Finder on their phone, straight after watching a short video, knowing little
about UEL.
• The same tool will be used for several years, as new courses open and dates and fees change.
• Only UEL can decide who gets a place and how much scholarship they receive. The Finder can never
promise a place, a scholarship amount, a visa or a job.
This is a mock project. The university and its public website are real, and you should use them for your
research. Everything else is illustrative.
Your tasks
Six tasks. Tasks 1 to 4 and 6 are written answers. Task 5 is a small working prototype.
1. Understanding the student. One or two pages. Describe the student and their parents as people, not
as users of a tool.
• What are they hoping for? What would success look like to the student, and to their family?
• What are they afraid of?
• What would make them choose UEL over another UK university, or over staying in India?

• Why would they trust a tool like this, or not? What would make them give up halfway?
• What would make them go on to actually apply?
2. Research. Gather the information the tool needs, from UEL’s website and the UK government website
(GOV.UK).
• At least 15 courses across different subjects: the fee, the campus, the start dates, the entry
requirements for students from India, and any extra conditions.
• UEL’s scholarship for international students, and how it works.
• The cost of living in London for a student, and any other first-year costs a family should know about.
Collect it by hand or with a scraper, a program that collects information from websites automatically (see
Tools you can use below), and tell us which you used and how. Every fact needs a link to where you found
it and the date you checked it. Tell us anything you found that is out of date, contradicts itself, or does not
apply to students from India.
3. The questions. Write every question the tool asks, in the order the student sees them. For each one,
give us:
• the question, exactly as the student reads it on screen;
• the answer format: choose one option, choose several options, a dropdown list, a slider or a typed
answer;
• every answer option, word for word;
• who sees it, if not everyone (for example, only students applying for a master’s);
• why you ask it, and what it changes in the student’s results.
Then show how the questions are grouped into screens, and tell us:
• the headline and button on the very first screen;
• how the student should feel by the time they reach their results, and how the order of your questions
builds up to that;
• where you ask for the student’s name and phone number, and why there;
• how the parents get involved;
• where the tool could mislead a student, and how you prevent it;
• what our adviser and UEL should be told about each student, and how you would decide which
students get a call first;
• how we could tell which video or influencer brought each student to the tool, and why that is worth
knowing.
4. The results page. Write the results page that each of these two students would see after answering the
questions: the exact words on screen, not a description of them. Its job is to make each student picture
themselves at UEL and want to go, while staying completely honest.
• Student A. Lives in Hyderabad. Has finished school (Class 12, CBSE board) with 82%. Wants to
study computing. Loves football. Has an education loan arranged. Wants to work in the UK after
graduating.

• Student B. Lives in Kochi. Aged 17 and still in Class 12 (CBSE board), expecting around 75%. Wants
to study business. Loves food and cooking. Their whole family will make the decision. Worried about
the cost.
Each results page should include:
• a headline;
• the course that suits them, and whether they appear to meet its entry requirements;
• the scholarship, and a realistic estimate of the total cost of the first year;
• the centre of the page: what their life as a student in London could look like, built from what they told
us about themselves, so that it feels like it was written for them;
• the next step, and the button that takes them there.
Keep each page under 300 words. Then write the WhatsApp message Student B could forward to their
parents, in under 120 words. Nothing can promise what only UEL decides: a place, a scholarship amount,
a visa or a job.
Under each piece, add a short note on why it would work on that person: which hopes or worries it speaks
to, and why it is in that order.
5. The prototype. A small working version of the tool, on a live link that opens on a phone.
• Five or six of your questions from task 3, leading to whether the student appears to meet the entry
requirements, their scholarship and their first-year cost, using your research from task 2.
• One of your results pages from task 4.
• The screen where the student gives their details, and what they see after that.
Build it however you like, with any tool. We are judging what it is like to use, not the code.
6. Judgement. Using your prototype or your questions from task 3, tell us what each of these people
would see, and whether that is right:
• a student still in Class 12 who chooses a master’s degree;
• a student whose marks are below the entry requirement;
• a commerce graduate (B.Com) who wants a master’s in computing;
• a parent who opens a link their child sent them.
Tools you can use
You do not need to learn new tools for this. A lot of what this assessment needs already exists as free,
open-source code on GitHub, and an AI coding tool such as Claude Code can set it up and run it for you.
Collecting the course information (task 2). You can copy facts by hand, or use a scraper to collect the
course pages in bulk. Scrapy is a free, open-source scraper: github.com/scrapy/scrapy. Give that link to
your AI coding tool, tell it which pages you want and what to collect from each, and let it install and run
Scrapy for you.
Scrape politely and anonymously: a few pages at a time, follow the site’s robots.txt rules, and never log in
or use a personal account. A scraper collects old and wrong information as happily as correct information,
so check every fact against the page itself.

What to submit
Submit all four to team@edin.world by the deadline above:

What we are looking for
• All six tasks fitting together as one tool, not six separate exercises.
• A real understanding of what these students and their parents want and fear, and writing that makes
them want it.
• A clear understanding of who makes the decision, and what would make them act.
• Research that is accurate, sourced and dated, including what you found to be wrong.
• A tool that works well on a phone, for someone who has just tapped through from a video.
• Honesty about the scholarship and the costs.
• Clear placeholders where something is missing, rather than made-up detail.
